Love With Condition I sat at the corner of our bed, scrolling through my phone. We had only one bedroom in our small house, so distance was never about space. It was about silence. My mother sat on the other side, watching me. I could feel it without looking. That familiar look. The one that had already decided what I was before asking who I was. To her, I was wasting time. Lost in a screen. Ignoring the family. Maybe she never thought that sometimes a phone feels safer than people. That sometimes strangers understand boundaries better than your own home. --- I kept scrolling, not really reading anything. My mind had already gone somewhere else. Back to all the times she cared. When I had a fever, she would sit beside me. "Did you take the medicine?" I would nod weakly. "Drink more water." A pause. Then—"How much of your class did you miss?" Even sick, I understood. My health mattered. But not by itself. It mattered because I needed to get back up. Needed to continue. Needed to perform. If I scored well, she would smile. "That's my child." If I failed? "What happened to you? You used to do better. Do you know how hard we work for you? Is this your effort in return for us?" Love always came with evaluation. I remembered another evening. I had sat beside her without my phone. She was folding clothes. Talking about relatives. "Your aunt's daughter got admission into engineering," she said. I nodded. "Hm." "She's such a hardworking girl." "Hm." "And that boy from next street? Already preparing for government exams." I listened anyway. Not because I cared. Because she cared. Because sometimes love is listening to conversations you'd never choose. Then once, I tried. "Amma, can I tell you something?" She didn't even look up. "Hm?" "I've been feeling really stressed lately." "Everyone feels stressed." "No, I mean—" "What stress do you even have? Your only job is studying." I laughed awkwardly. Not because it was funny. But because what else do you do when your feelings are dismissed before they fully exist? Another time. "Amma, I don't think I want—" She cut me off. "You think too much." "I'm serious." "You're too young to know what's right." And another. "I actually read about this—" "Oh? Now the phone teaches you more than your parents?" I stopped bringing things up after that. Not suddenly. Slowly. Like someone learning not to touch a hot surface. Her voice pulled me back. "Can't you stop that?" I looked up. She was staring at my phone. "You spend enough time on that thing." I locked it immediately. "Sorry." "Every time I see you, you're just staring at that screen." I moved closer. She adjusted her saree and sighed. "Learn to spend time with family." I swallowed. Because wasn't I trying now? Then she asked— "Why don't you talk to us anymore?" For a second, I almost laughed. Not because it was funny. But because the irony hurt. I looked at her. Did she genuinely not know? "Nothing," I said quietly. She frowned. "Nothing? There has to be something." "No." "Then why are you always distant?" She kept going. "Earlier, you used to tell me everything." Did I? Or did I just speak before I learned what happened when I did? Because when I talk, you correct me. Because when I feel something inconvenient, it becomes disrespect. Because when I disagree, I become immature. Because every honest conversation somehow becomes about how wrong I am. Because I got tired. But I already knew the ending. "You're just a child." "You'll understand when you grow up." "We only want what's best for you." So instead, I said— "I don't know." After a long pause, she said quietly— "You think I don't love you?" That hurt. Because that was never the question. "I know you love me." "Then why are you acting like this?" Because love and understanding are not the same thing. Because care is not always comfort. Because being fed, protected, and provided for doesn't automatically mean being heard. But how do you explain that to someone who hears pain as accusation? So I said the safest thing. "I'm fine." My mother loves me. I know she does. She has shown it in all the ways she knows how. In medicine. In reminders. In sacrifices. In worry. by ravi krishna
